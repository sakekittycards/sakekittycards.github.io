Should You Grade Your Pokémon Card?

Grading is an investment with a fee, a wait, and an uncertain outcome. Here is the arithmetic, including a number almost nobody publishes: what a PSA 10 is actually worth relative to a 9.

The three numbers that decide it

Grading is worth it when the graded card is worth more than the raw card plus the fee, adjusted for the odds of getting the grade you are hoping for. That is the whole model. It needs three inputs: what the card is worth raw, what it is worth at each grade, and what it costs to find out.

The cost is the easy one. PSA’s entry tier has recently sat around $25 per card, and you are shipping in both directions with insurance. Call it about $30 per card all-in as a working floor. Grading fees change, so check PSA’s current published pricing before you commit to a submission.

The raw value you can look up in about twenty seconds with the card lookup tool. The hard input is the third one, and it is where most people guess.

What a PSA 10 is actually worth versus a Grade 9

This is our own measurement, not a repeated internet figure. Here is the method and the data.

You will see a lot of confident claims about this ratio online, most of them repeating a single unsourced figure. So we measured it. Taking every Pokémon card where both a Grade 9 price and a PSA 10 price exist — 58,533 matched pairs — here is the actual distribution of the PSA 10 to Grade 9 ratio. Full method is at the bottom of this section; you can reproduce it.

Method, sample and limitations +

Source. The full Pokémon per-grade price file we license from PriceCharting, re-downloaded and re-measured on 1 September 2026. That file held 92,785 product rows on the day of measurement.

What was matched. Every row carrying both a Grade 9 price and a PSA 10 price above zero — 58,533 rows. The ratio is computed per card and then the median is taken across cards; it is not a ratio of two medians, which would be dominated by a handful of expensive cards.

An asymmetry worth knowing. PriceCharting’s Grade 9 column is a generic Grade 9 — PSA, BGS and CGC blended — while the PSA 10 column is specifically PSA 10. So this is a PSA-10-to-Grade-9 ratio. A strict PSA-10-to-PSA-9 ratio would likely run slightly higher, since PSA slabs carry a resale premium at the same nominal grade.

Era definitions. Taken from each card’s release date, not from set names: vintage = released 2003 or earlier, modern = 2020 or later, middle = in between. Vintage and modern together are only 53% of the sample. The middle era is the single largest bucket and medians below modern, so the fat multiple is specific to genuine vintage rather than to "older cards" generally. 3,573 rows (6.1%) carry no release date and appear only in the ALL row.

Exclusions. 48 rows were dropped where the PSA 10 priced below its own Grade 9 or above 100× it. Those are data artifacts — a stale one-off sale or a mispriced row — not market signal.

Known limitations. These are PriceCharting’s modelled prices, not our own transaction record, so any error in their inputs is inherited. Thin-volume cards have noisy prices; the volume-weighted column exists to show what happens when you reweight toward cards that actually change hands. And the spread widens sharply with value — in the $250+ band the middle half of cards runs 2.3× to 7.0×, so the median there describes the population, not your particular card.

Reproduce it. The measurement script is scripts/verify_psa_multiple.py in our site repository. It prints the table above. If the script and this page ever disagree, the script is right and this page is stale.

Three things fall out of this that change how you should think about submitting:

  • Modern is remarkably predictable. The middle half of modern cards sits between 2.6× and 3.3×. If you are grading a 2020-or-later card, 3× is a safe planning number — and that tightness is the useful part, because it means the estimate is worth trusting.
  • Only genuine vintage breaks the pattern. Pre-2004 medians 4.42× with a volume-weighted 5.40×, so a vintage card justifies grading at a materially lower raw value. But "old" is not the same as vintage: the 2004–2019 middle era medians 2.89×, slightly below modern. A 2011 card is not a vintage card.
  • The multiple climbs with the card’s value, and so does the uncertainty. The $250+ band medians 4.02×, but its middle half spans 2.3× to 7.0×. On expensive cards the average stops being a useful prediction for your specific card.
  • The commonly repeated 6.8× figure is a tail value, not an average. It exists, but it lives out at the extreme of the distribution. Planning a submission around it is how people end up disappointed.

Running the numbers on your card

Take a modern card worth $120 raw in Near Mint. Assume the Grade 9 sells for $150 and, at the measured modern 3×, the PSA 10 sells for around $450.

  • It comes back a 10: $450 minus $30 in fees = $420 against $120 raw. Excellent.
  • It comes back a 9: $150 minus $30 = $120 against $120 raw. You broke even and waited two months.
  • It comes back an 8: you are now underwater against simply selling it raw.

So the entire decision on that card rests on one question: how likely is the 10? Not “is it a nice card” — how likely is the 10. And the honest answer for most people, on most cards, is that they do not know, because the things that cost a 10 are centering measured to the millimeter, print lines, edge whitening only visible at an angle, and surface imperfections you cannot see without a light and a loupe.

That is the entire reason pre-grading screening exists. Roughly $3 to $5 per card to find out which cards are worth a $30 gamble is a much better bet than $30 per card to find out the hard way.

When not to grade

The card is cheap

A $30 fee on a $15 card cannot be rescued by any grade. Bulk-grading low-value cards is the most common way people lose money in this hobby.

You already know it has a flaw

A visible ding, a soft corner, obvious off-centering. You are paying $30 to have someone confirm what you can already see.

You need the money now

Grading turnaround is measured in weeks to months. If you are selling because you need the cash, the raw sale today beats a better price later.

It is sentimental

Grade it anyway if you want it protected and displayed. Just do not pretend that is an investment decision — it is a nice reason and it does not need a spreadsheet.

Grading questions

What does it cost to grade a Pokemon card? +
At PSA's entry-level tier the fee has recently sat around $25 per card, before shipping in both directions and before insurance. Budget roughly $30 per card all-in as a working floor, and check PSA's current published pricing before you submit, because grading fees change.
What raw value makes grading worth it? +
As a rough gate: modern cards need to be worth enough that a 10 covers the fee and beats the raw card, which in practice means a raw value in the low hundreds unless you are very confident of the grade. Vintage justifies grading at lower raw values because the PSA 10 multiple is larger.
How much more is a PSA 10 worth than a Grade 9? +
Across 58,533 Pokemon cards where both a Grade 9 and a PSA 10 price exist, the median ratio is 3.00x. Modern cards from 2020 onward cluster tightly around 2.98x. Pre-2004 vintage runs a median 4.42x and a volume-weighted 5.40x, because the vintage distribution has a much fatter tail. Note this is PSA 10 against a generic Grade 9, not specifically a PSA 9.
Is it worth grading a card I am not confident is a 10? +
Usually not, and this is where most money is lost. You are not buying a PSA 10, you are buying a lottery ticket on one. If the card comes back a 9 you have spent about $30 to end up with an asset worth roughly a third of what you were hoping for. Screening the card first is how you avoid paying that tuition repeatedly.
Does it matter which grading company I use? +
It matters to resale liquidity more than to accuracy. PSA slabs have the deepest sold-comp history for Pokemon, which makes them the easiest to price and sell. See our PSA vs CGC vs BGS comparison.
