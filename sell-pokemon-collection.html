First, the honest part: nobody pays market value

Including us. Here is why, so you can judge any offer — ours or anyone else’s — on something better than a gut feeling.

Every offer you get will sit below what the cards would fetch sold individually online. That is not a trick, it is the whole business model. A buyer is paying you today, in one payment, with no fees on your side, no listings to write, no packages to mail and no returns to handle — and then carrying the cost and the months of work of selling those cards one at a time.

So the question is never “is this offer below market?” It always is. The real question is how far below, and can the buyer show you the math? That is why our whole rate ladder is published further down this page instead of being quoted case by case.

If it turns out you would genuinely do better selling a few cards yourself, we will tell you that. On a handful of expensive cards it is often true. On nine hundred cards it almost never is — the per-sale fees and the hours swamp the difference. The full breakdown of dealer percentages is here if you want the numbers behind that.

The four mistakes that cost sellers the most

We see these constantly, and every one of them is avoidable in an afternoon.

1

Letting the good cards get bulked

The most expensive mistake by far. If a $180 alt art disappears into a 3,000-card bulk count, you were paid about four cents for it. Pull out every holo, full art, alt art, vintage card and slab before anyone quotes you — even the ones you are unsure about. Being wrong about a card costs you nothing. Missing one costs you the card.

2

Pricing off listings instead of sales

A $600 listing does not mean the card is worth $600. It means one person is asking $600 and nobody has paid it. On a low-volume card, a single fantasy listing can sit at the top of the results for a year. Filter to completed sales. Every serious buyer prices off sold data, so if you price off listings you will think every honest offer is an insult.

3

Over-grading your own condition

Sellers habitually rate their own cards a full tier high. A card with edge whitening on the back and slightly soft corners is not Near Mint, and on our ladder that is 15% of the value gone at Lightly Played, 30% at Moderately Played. Here is how to read them honestly before you set your expectations.

4

Shipping badly

Cards that arrive dinged get quoted as dinged cards. A loose stack in a bubble mailer will shift in transit and the corners take it. This is pure avoidable loss — the packing guide covers the method by lot size.

Five steps, and you can stop at any of them.

What a real offer looks like

Use this to judge us as much as anyone else.

  • It is itemized. You should see which cards were valued individually, at what value, at what condition, and what rate was applied. One number for 2,000 cards with no breakdown is the warning sign — not the number itself.
  • It flags things against the buyer’s own interest. If something in your collection is worth more than we want to pay for it, we say so. We work the same Florida shows every month; word travels faster than any single good deal is worth.
  • The percentage scales with value. One flat rate applied to a $5 card and a $900 card is either generous on one end or robbery on the other.
  • It is free and reversible. Nothing ships before you agree a number, and if you decline a final offer on cards you already mailed, they come back to you. Ask any buyer that question specifically before you send them anything.
  • You are encouraged to get a second offer. Always do. Tell us you are doing it; that is normal and it does not offend anyone who prices honestly.

Cash or store credit?

Credit pays about ten percentage points more than cash on the same cards, and at the top tier it reaches 95% of market value against 85% for cash. The reason is simple: credit stays in the business, so it costs us less to give you.

Which means the decision is not really about the percentage. It is about whether you were going to spend that money on cards anyway. Consolidating a collection and planning to buy something better with the proceeds? Credit is straightforwardly the better deal. Leaving the hobby? Take the cash and do not let a bigger number talk you into a currency you cannot spend.

Collection selling questions

Do I have to list every card to get an offer on a collection? +
No. That is the whole reason this page exists. For a shoebox, a longbox, a binder run, or an inherited collection, send photos and a rough description and we will work from that. The card-by-card lookup tool on the Sell / Trade page is there for people who want to itemize a short list of specific cards — it is not the right tool for 4,000 commons.
How big does a collection have to be? +
There is no minimum. We buy single binders and we buy garage-full estate collections. What changes with size is the process: small lots we can quote from photos, large lots usually get sorted in person or after they arrive.
What if the collection has non-Pokémon cards mixed in? +
Tell us what is in there. Pokémon is what we specialize in and what we pay the most for. We will be straight with you about anything outside that rather than quietly valuing it at zero.
Do you buy collections you have to travel for? +
For large Florida collections, yes — we already drive the state every month for shows, so we can often work a pickup into a route we are already running. Ask.
What happens if I do not like the offer? +
Nothing. The offer is free and there is no obligation. If you mailed cards in and you turn the offer down, we ship them back to you — we are not going to hold your collection hostage over a number you did not agree to.
How many offers should I get? +
At least two, and tell each buyer you are getting others — that is normal and it does not offend anyone who prices honestly. What you are checking is not just whose number is highest, but which buyer will actually show you how they got there.
Should I sort the collection before sending it? +
Pull out anything obviously valuable — holos, full arts, vintage, anything already slabbed — so those get priced individually instead of vanishing into a bulk count. Beyond that, don’t spend a weekend sorting commons by set. It will not change the bulk rate.
How do you decide what a collection is worth? +
Every card with real value is priced off recent sold data for that exact card, set, number and printing — not off the highest asking price someone has posted. Then the published rate ladder applies. Bulk is priced by category or by weight. We show our work if you ask.
